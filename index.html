About & methods

Question. Where is Łódź hottest on clear summer days, how much of that is explained by vegetation and by built-up land, and which places are hotter than those two would predict?

  1. Surface temperature — Landsat 8 and 9 Collection 2 surface temperature (thermal band, 30 m) on every June–August day from 2018 to 2025 on which at least 90% of the city was cloud-free; clouds, shadows and water are masked.
  2. Surface heat — each cell's temperature minus the city median of the same day, so that hot and cool days can be combined; the map shows the mean over all clear days.
  3. Vegetation — NDVI from the same Landsat scenes (red and near-infrared), so greenery and temperature are observed at the same moment. Sentinel-2 (10 m) is used as an independent cross-check.
  4. Built-up share — the share of the cell classified as built-up in ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m).
  5. Grid and pixels — the map shows the 30 m satellite pixels or square 500 m cells (UTM zone 34N, kept when at least half lies inside the city); each cell is the average of its pixels. The statistics use the cells.
  6. Statistics — linear regression of surface heat on vegetation and built-up share, checked by spatially blocked cross-validation (2 km blocks); Moran's I for spatial clustering; Getis–Ord Gi* hot and cold spots with a 5% false-discovery rate.

Reading the map. Land surface temperature is the temperature of roofs, roads and vegetation seen from space around 11:35 local time — not the air temperature people feel, although the two are related. Hotter than expected compares each cell with what its vegetation and built-up share predict; cells more than 1 °C hotter point to heat from roofs, paving or industry that greenery alone does not explain.

The full method, results and limitations are in the paper; every number in this app is produced by the open Python pipeline in the repository.
